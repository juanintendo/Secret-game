## 1. What this project is trying to become

A premium-feeling, story-driven tactical RPG about three women who inherit the wreckage of a once-perfect combat team. None is the leader. Their rivalry, distrust, decisions, and battlefield actions gradually turn three dangerous individuals into a single lethal unit.

Its playable identity is the intersection of:

- character-scale tactical combat with elevation, facing, positioning, cover, reactions, and destructible opportunities;
- an animated graphic-novel layer in which decisions change missions, loyalties, capabilities, and consequences;
- authored anime spectacle used at high-value dramatic moments;
- an extremely clean 1990s techno-anime visual language rendered at modern fidelity.

This is not “XCOM with an anime skin,” and it is not a visual novel interrupted by unrelated battles. Narrative decisions must alter tactical state, and tactical outcomes must alter narrative state.

## 2. Founding pillars

### 2.1 The fixed trio matters

The three reference characters are the dramatic and mechanical center. A fixed trio lets us author stronger relationships, battlefield banter, combination abilities, injuries, betrayals, and cutscene choreography than a disposable army of interchangeable units.

Possible supporting recruits can be explored later, but the first vertical slice assumes three controllable protagonists.

### 2.2 Every decision creates playable consequences

A meaningful choice changes at least one of:

- mission objective;
- deployment position or available route;
- faction disposition;
- information available to the player;
- a protagonist's trust, stress, wound, or ability;
- an encounter, boss phase, rescue, loss, or future scene.

Purely cosmetic dialogue choices are allowed for character expression, but they must be labeled internally as expression rather than consequence.

### 2.3 Combat is legible before it is spectacular

The player should understand danger, cover, range, height, line of sight, and likely consequences before committing. Animation celebrates a clear decision; it never conceals the rules.

### 2.4 Modern clarity, analog soul

The base render is clean. The 90s feeling comes from composition, color separation, held animation, painted light, CRT motifs, optical artifacts, typography, sound, and editorial rhythm—not from smearing noise across every pixel.

### 2.5 Authored imperfection, zero accidental slop

Hand-painted asymmetry and analog artifacts are intentional. Extra fingers, drifting faces, inconsistent costumes, changing limb lengths, unreadable silhouettes, and fake text are defects.

## 4. Combat hypothesis

Do not stack every mechanic from Final Fantasy Tactics and XCOM. The first coherent ruleset to test is:

- square or diamond grid with discrete elevation;
- three protagonists deployed together;
- interleaved character initiative rather than full-team turns;
- two action points per activation;
- movement, primary action, utility action, guard/overwatch, and character ability;
- half/full cover where architecture supports it;
- facing, flank, height, and line-of-sight advantages;
- deterministic base damage with clearly surfaced hit or mitigation uncertainty;
- reactions and interrupts with visible triggers;
- environmental actions: doors, terminals, hazards, power, destructible cover;
- objectives beyond elimination;
- fail-forward mission outcomes where appropriate.

The first prototype must answer four questions:

1. Is controlling only three deeply authored units tactically rich enough?
2. Does interleaved initiative create good rescue, combo, and interruption decisions?
3. Can cover and elevation coexist without making every map visually noisy?
4. Can a player forecast outcomes without opening a spreadsheet?

## 5. The trio as a combat system

The protagonists should not merely have three damage flavors. Each should own a tactical verb.

Current role hypothesis:

| Protagonist | Tactical verb | Battlefield expression |
| --- | --- | --- |
| White-haired human | Redirect / Exploit | Reposition attention, traverse danger, create flanks, manipulate, and capitalize on openings |
| Cyborg mech pilot | Disrupt / Break | Tank, breach, counter, pin, crowd-control, or deliver heavy damage through the only mech |
| Luminous synthetic | Rewrite / Conduct | Invade systems, weaponize lightning, manipulate conditions and initiative, and create earned support windows |

No member is the permanent leader. Tactical authorship is distributed among the player and the trio's evolving trust.

The trio's combination grammar is also its character arc. At first, each woman optimizes her own turn, competes for control, interrupts the others, and may waste openings. As trust develops, they unlock assists, handoffs, reactions, rescues, chained movement, and ultimately synchronized limit breaks.

Progression therefore has two dimensions:

- **individual power:** each protagonist's equipment, abilities, mech configuration, and mastery;
- **unit cohesion:** what the three can only do because they understand and trust one another.

The survivors begin the main timeline with skill and experience, but without their old synchronization. The new recruit is not a level-one weakling; the unit is level one.

## 6. Narrative structure

Use a stateful episode model rather than an exponentially branching tree.

- Choices set facts, relationships, debts, knowledge, wounds, and faction pressure.
- Multiple paths can reconverge at an event while preserving different state and consequences.
- Combat results write back to the same state store.
- The mission briefing should reflect what the player actually knows, not omniscient exposition.
- Some consequences should be immediate; others should mature one or two missions later.

Recommended authoring layer: Ink integrated into Unity. Ink is text-first, versionable, debuggable, supports branching logic, and has an official Unity integration. The game owns presentation, state validation, tactical commands, and save migration; Ink owns authored narrative flow.

### 6.1 Canonical prologue structure

The tutorial opens years before the main campaign with a different version of the team:

- the cyborg mech pilot;
- the luminous synthetic;
- a third human woman who is the team's established leader;
- a female remote operator who delivers briefings and tactical support from headquarters.

The operator has a controlled, disembodied presence: dry, unnervingly composed, observant, and institutionally embedded. “GLaDOS-like” describes her dramatic function and psychological pressure, not a direct imitation of GLaDOS's voice or jokes.

The original leader is fully human but not low-tech. She uses an independent AI copilot in real time for threat analysis, tactical simulation, probability, targeting, and decision support. Her defining ability is not obedience to superior computation; it is judgment. She can distinguish between a recommendation that is statistically optimal and a decision that is wrong in context, then override it without hesitation. She leads because she accepts responsibility for that judgment.

The leader's AI copilot and Operator are separate entities with different authority:

- the copilot advises the leader locally and has no command authority;
- Operator briefs, coordinates, and connects the distributed operation;
- the human leader makes final field decisions.

Headquarters has no known physical location. The field team—including its leader—does not know where it is. Communications pass through blinded, compartmentalized relays. Therefore, the attack cannot be explained by the leader being followed or carelessly revealing a location. The antagonist has penetrated something deeper: Operator's infrastructure, the relay chain, the synchronization architecture, or an internal trust boundary.

The tutorial team begins near maximum power and in extraordinary synchronization. This is an intentional playable promise of what the game can eventually become. The mission appears to be a showcase victory:

1. The trio executes clean handoffs and advanced combinations.
2. Team play fills a shared synchronization/limit resource.
3. Their first synchronized limit break is spectacular and succeeds.
4. They approach the final objective and prepare a second limit break.
5. The cyborg and synthetic suffer simultaneous system failure from a massive hostile intrusion.
6. The apparent mission is revealed as bait and payback: the real antagonist used the operation to expose the team at maximum synchronization while striking the hidden headquarters and/or its communications infrastructure.
7. Corrupted comms leak live audio from HQ—gunfire, resistance, a confrontation, and the operator apparently being killed.
8. The battlefield collapses into misinformation, disabled systems, broken coordination, and enemy pressure. The leader's copilot also presents compromised or catastrophically narrow recommendations.
9. The human leader recognizes that the machine-optimal answer is wrong, rejects it, and preserves her own agency. This decision is what the attacker failed to model.
10. She extracts or protects the other two and remains behind to cover their escape.
11. The cyborg and synthetic escape immediately before the mission site is destroyed. The destruction makes the leader's death overwhelmingly likely, but no body or definitive proof survives.
12. Communications are lost completely. Operator is presumed destroyed at HQ; the leader is presumed killed at the mission site.

The prologue must end on loss, not a simple cinematic power reset. The player has personally experienced perfect unity before being forced to live with its absence.

The double destruction is important and must remain geographically legible: HQ and the field site are separate unknown locations attacked as parts of the same operation.

### 6.2 Main-timeline inciting incident

The surviving cyborg and synthetic cannot verify that the attack ended with the destruction of the two sites. They assume the operation may still be hunting them. They separate, erase or abandon old identities, minimize network exposure, and remain underground for years. Their survival is not peace; it is prolonged uncertainty.

Years later, the same operator re-enters their lives.

Her apparent return is possible because she continuously backed up her identity, operational state, and personal data across distributed infrastructure. This creates a central uncertainty rather than an easy resurrection:

- Is continuity of data continuity of person?
- What was the timestamp of the surviving backup?
- What did she lose?
- Could the attacker have altered, forked, or deliberately released her?
- Why did she wait years?
- Does she remember her own apparent death?

Her return is not a casual call and she does not simply issue a reunion order. Reaching each survivor is a planned operation adapted to that woman's hiding strategy, history, fears, and technical vulnerabilities. Operator must prove enough private continuity to earn attention without demanding blind trust.

She maneuvers the survivors toward reforming the team as part of a larger plan. The main game initially begins with the cyborg and synthetic alone. They later recruit the white-haired human for a reason that Operator refuses or is unable to reveal immediately.

The new trio has no appointed leader. The two survivors compare the recruit—fairly or unfairly—to the missing woman. The recruit resists being treated as a replacement. Early missions should allow their competition and mistrust to create mechanical friction without making the player feel arbitrarily sabotaged.

### 6.3 Mystery stack established by the opening

The prologue creates five long-running questions:

1. Who engineered the trap and what was actually taken or installed at HQ?
2. What happened to the former leader after communications failed?
3. Is the returned operator the same individual, an altered backup, a fork, or a weapon?
4. Why is the white-haired human uniquely required?
5. Why could the enemy compromise both synthetic systems at precisely the second limit-break event?

These mysteries should share causal machinery. Five unrelated surprise answers would create plot soup.

### 6.4 Rules for the operator's return

The operator's backup mechanism must have limitations so death retains weight:

- backups are timestamped rather than magical continuous immortality;
- restoration loses some state, memory, embodiment, access, or legal authority;
- cryptographic identity can prove provenance but not subjective continuity;
- distributed copies create the possibility of forks;
- a restored operator may distrust evidence produced by her own compromised infrastructure.

The player should be able to believe she is both their most important ally and a plausible extension of the original trap.

### 6.5 Recontact design principles

Operator contacts the survivors separately. Each recontact should function as a playable character introduction and security negotiation, not exposition.

For the cyborg mech pilot, a strong provisional approach is physical and mechanical:

- Operator reaches a dormant, isolated subsystem or maintenance artifact associated with the mech rather than the cyborg's body;
- the message contains knowledge only the old team should possess, but also a challenge that lets the cyborg independently authenticate it;
- following the trail requires the cyborg to risk exposing the mech or leaving a carefully maintained refuge;
- Operator earns the next minute of attention rather than immediate loyalty.

For the luminous synthetic, the contact should deliberately respect the trauma of the hack:

- Operator does not enter or speak through the synthetic's internal systems;
- she uses an external analog, optical, acoustic, or otherwise air-gapped channel the synthetic can inspect before accepting;
- the message demonstrates knowledge of the synthetic as a person rather than merely knowledge of her architecture;
- the synthetic's first decision is whether to observe, decode, destroy, or follow the signal.

The two contact paths can converge on the same first mission without either survivor initially knowing the other has been contacted. Their reunion then becomes an event Operator has engineered, and possibly the first reason to doubt her intentions.

Exact contact mechanisms remain provisional until each survivor's underground life and personal history are defined.

## 7. Visual production model

### 7.1 2.5D world

- 3D tactical environments for camera freedom, elevation, cover, lighting, destruction, and pathfinding.
- Toon-shaded 3D protagonists during gameplay, built to match strict 2D character bibles.
- Authored 2D portraits, reaction inserts, impact frames, choice tableaux, and UI illustrations.
- Selective 2D-over-3D effects for speed lines, energy, smoke accents, eye cuts, and graphic transitions.

### 7.2 Animation cadence

- Gameplay locomotion prioritizes responsiveness and silhouette.
- Character acting can use stepped/held keys to evoke cel animation.
- Impact frames, smears, and short bursts can temporarily break cadence.
- UI remains fluid and responsive; it must not inherit fake low frame rate.
- Cutscenes choose cadence shot by shot rather than imposing one global “anime FPS.”

### 7.3 Analog treatment

Build a controlled post stack with independently tunable passes:

- subtle scanline or phosphor texture;
- luma/chroma separation;
- restrained bloom and halation;
- line-weight stabilization;
- slight gate weave only in authored sequences;
- optional tape dropout used as punctuation;
- palette-aware grain;
- crisp UI exclusion mask.

No global VHS preset. No permanent chromatic blur. No grain strong enough to erase illustration or tactical information.

## 8. UI direction

The interface should have Persona-level authorship without borrowing Persona 5's exact red/black collage language.

Working direction: **broadcast-control interface**.

- Menus assemble like routed video signals and mechanical switchers.
- Character panels behave like selected channels, with distinct framing and motion signatures.
- Wipes, hard cuts, sync pulses, masks, and scan reveals replace generic fades.
- Tactical overlays use cyan/green for information, magenta/coral for intent or character emphasis, red for irreversible danger, bone white for high-legibility text, and black for structure.
- Angles and asymmetry are used to direct the eye, not applied to every rectangle.
- Combat HUD is calm at rest and expressive on commitment.
- Choice UI previews the category of consequence without spoiling its exact result.

The UI must survive keyboard/mouse and controller navigation from its first real implementation.

## 9. Cutscene tiers

“Brutal anime cutscenes” require production triage, not lower ambition.

| Tier | Use | Production form |
| --- | --- | --- |
| S | Opening, chapter climax, irreversible reveal | Fully authored anime sequence or exceptionally polished hybrid sequence |
| A | Mission opening/ending, boss entrance, major relationship turn | In-engine cinematic with toon characters, bespoke shots, 2D inserts, strong compositing |
| B | Most conversations, investigations, briefings | Animated graphic novel: layered art, limited character animation, camera, lighting, particles, typography |
| C | Reactive battlefield dialogue | Portrait/cut-in, voice, concise motion language |

The vertical slice needs one short example of S/A ambition and one B scene. It does not need five minutes of bespoke animation.

## 10. Engine decision

### Recommendation: Unity 6, pinned supported 6000.x version, URP

Why it fits:

- C# and text-authored systems are highly workable for Astra and normal code review.
- URP is enough for a stylized 2.5D render and custom full-screen effects without paying the complexity cost of a photoreal pipeline.
- Cinemachine and Timeline cover camera direction and sequencing.
- UI Toolkit is a strong candidate for menus and authoring tools; combat/world UI can be evaluated against uGUI where necessary.
- ScriptableObjects can hold immutable definitions, while JSON/Ink and explicit runtime state keep narrative and balance data inspectable.
- Strong editor scripting lets Astra generate validators, importers, test scenes, and repeatable asset setup instead of manually clicking through the editor.
- Current Unity Personal terms are viable for early indie development, subject to the current revenue/funding threshold.

### Why not Unreal as the default

Unreal offers the strongest out-of-box cinematic ceiling, but a large share of production truth tends to accumulate inside binary assets, Blueprints, materials, and editor-authored sequences. That weakens diffability and makes agent-led iteration harder to inspect. It remains a valid fallback if the first visual spike proves Unity cannot meet the target without unreasonable custom work.

### Why not Godot as the default

Godot is excellent for text-friendly scenes, fast iteration, and ownership. For this particular target, we would assume more responsibility for high-end cinematic tooling, console path, animation pipeline, and specialized render polish. It is the cleanest architecture choice but not the lowest production-risk choice.

### Decision gate

The engine is provisional until a two-week technical art spike proves all four:

1. tactical camera and grid readability;
2. one protagonist matching the visual target in motion;
3. the analog post stack without muddying UI;
4. an animated transition from narrative scene into combat.

If Unity fails the visual target, compare the same shot in Unreal before building more game systems.

## 11. Technical architecture for agent-led development

Keep production truth inspectable.

- `Game.Core`: deterministic rules and shared primitives, without Unity scene dependencies where practical.
- `Game.Tactics`: grid, navigation, line of sight, cover, actions, initiative, reactions, objectives.
- `Game.Narrative`: Ink bridge, story facts, consequences, relationship state, save migration.
- `Game.Presentation`: cameras, animation events, VFX, audio cues, cut-ins, screen treatment.
- `Game.UI`: input-independent view models plus display implementations.
- `Game.Content`: definitions and validated content schemas.
- `Game.Editor`: importers, generators, validators, preview tools, screenshot harness.
- `Game.Tests`: deterministic simulations, narrative state tests, content validation, golden screenshots.

Rules:

- Gameplay logic never depends on a Timeline clip.
- Narrative commands request typed game actions rather than invoking scene objects by fragile string names.
- Definitions are immutable at runtime; mutable campaign state is separate and versioned.
- Randomness is seeded and logged for replayable bugs.
- Every mission can boot directly into a developer test harness.
- Binary editor assets are kept thin; important values are mirrored or generated from reviewable source data where sensible.

## 12. Anti-slop production gate

No generated or externally sourced visual becomes canonical merely because it looks impressive once.

Each character asset must pass:

- identity match against the approved model sheet;
- front/profile/back proportions;
- costume and material continuity;
- correct limb, hand, finger, and accessory topology;
- silhouette read at gameplay scale;
- face consistency across neutral, extreme, and three-quarter views;
- palette check under neutral light;
- deformation test for shoulders, elbows, wrists, hips, knees, and mouth;
- temporal continuity across an animation, not only selected frames;
- provenance and license record.

Each UI screen must pass:

- hierarchy at a two-second glance;
- keyboard/controller focus path;
- 16:9 safe-area test at minimum and maximum supported resolution;
- contrast and text-size check;
- no fake, malformed, or rasterized essential text;
- screenshot comparison against the selected target.

The final commercial vertical slice should be 15–20 minutes and replayable, containing one meaningful branch. Before that, the prologue itself becomes the first major playable production target because it demonstrates the end-state fantasy and the wound that drives the campaign.

### Prologue slice

1. Briefing from the operator with the original human leader present.
2. A controlled combat opening with all three veterans near maximum capability.
3. Two or three authored handoffs teach the synchronization system without exposing an endgame-sized ability menu.
4. The first synchronized limit break succeeds spectacularly.
5. The player builds toward a second limit break and chooses its target or setup.
6. The hostile intrusion occurs at commitment, compromising cyborg, synthetic, comms, and HQ.
7. The leader's AI copilot recommends a machine-optimal response that would sacrifice or abandon the compromised members.
8. The leader rejects the recommendation; this human override is the decisive act that creates an escape path.
9. The player retains limited control during the collapse rather than watching everything fail in a cutscene.
10. The old leader's final playable objective is evacuation/cover, not victory.
11. The two survivors escape as the field site is destroyed; the separate hidden HQ also falls silent.
12. Years underground pass before carefully individualized contacts reveal Operator's survival and begin the two-survivor chapter.

The tutorial must curate the veterans' available abilities. Showing the full future skill tree would overload a new player and make the later reset feel punitive. We demonstrate sophistication through choreography and authored combinations, not twenty buttons.

### Representative main-game slice

1. A Tier B animated graphic-novel scene introduces the trio and a decision.
2. The decision changes the mission's deployment, objective, information, or ally state.
3. A compact map teaches movement, cover, height, and one verb from each protagonist.
4. A mid-mission consequence demonstrates narrative ↔ combat feedback.
5. A three-phase climax uses a combination ability and one environmental interaction.
6. A 20–40 second Tier A/S-quality payoff demonstrates the cinematic promise.
7. An epilogue reflects both the earlier decision and combat outcome.

Success is not “lots of content.” Success is that a player can describe the game without naming Final Fantasy Tactics, XCOM, Persona, or a reference anime.
